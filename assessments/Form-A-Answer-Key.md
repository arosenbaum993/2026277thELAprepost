## Scoring rules for multi-point items

Apply these consistently. They are the same on both forms.

| Item type | 2 points | 1 point | 0 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EBSR / Multiple-part** (Part A + Part B) | Both parts correct | Exactly one part correct | Neither part correct |
| **Multiple-select** ("Select TWO") | Both correct choices marked, no incorrect choices marked | Exactly one correct choice marked and no more than one incorrect choice marked | Any other response, **including marking three or more choices** |
| **Match** (item 22, four cells) | All four cells correct | Exactly three cells correct | Two or fewer cells correct |
| **Extended writing** (item 39) | See `rubrics/Writing-Rubric-and-Scoring-Guide.md` | | |

**Enter every item's score in the reporting workbook, not just the total.** Item-level data is what produces the category profile and the intervention groups. A total score alone tells you nothing you can teach from.

# PART 1 — Items 1–10

---

### Item 1 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.1.a · DOK 2 · I-TEC

**B.** *He values winning more than he respects the warning he is given.* Nico answers a direct safety instruction with "Low is slow" and then "I didn't build a year of your life into a drone so I could come in second" — he treats the warning as an obstacle to winning rather than as information.

| Distractor | Why students choose it | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| A | Confuses rejecting *advice* with distrusting *skill*. Nico praises the frame ("the way a good frame should"). | Reads the conflict as interpersonal rather than as a character trait |
| C | Imports a stock "nervous competitor" schema not present in the text. | Relying on genre expectation over textual evidence |
| D | Over-reads the finality of the exchange. | Confusing tone with plot information |

---

### Item 2 — Key: **Part A = B · Part B = C** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.1.b · DOK 3 · I-TEC

**Part A — B, flashback.** Paragraph 6 begins "I remember the night he first showed me the drawings. I was eleven." The narrative moves backward from the race-day frame.

**Part B — C.** *"Then it is not strong. It is patient. It waits."* This directly predicts the failure mechanism: the frame gives way after eleven seconds in warm air.

*Note:* Part A distractor **A (foreshadowing)** is the most common error and is diagnostically useful — the paragraph *contains* foreshadowing, but the *device structuring the paragraph* is flashback. Students who choose A for Part A and C for Part B understand foreshadowing but have not distinguished the two devices; give them the point for Part B and address the distinction directly.

---

### Item 3 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.L.V.3.b · DOK 2 · I-LNG

**D.** *a moving current of air.* The clue is in the next paragraph, not the same sentence: "It came off the black runway in a long invisible column… Warm air. A river of it, going up." Students must read beyond the sentence — that is the Grade 7 step up from Grade 6.

Distractor **C** (*a slow, gradual change*) is the most common; it is a real meaning of *drift* and is defeated only by the surrounding context. Students choosing C are relying on a stored definition rather than the passage.

---

### Item 4 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.T.SS.2.a · DOK 2 · I-CSS

**C.** *tense, unforgiving.* "Cold," "no give," and "nothing in it at all" carry hardness and absence. The comparison arrives immediately before the warning scene and prepares the reader for a sky that will not forgive an error.

---

### Item 5 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.1.c · DOK 2 · I-TEC

**A.** *Skill without respect for limits can cost a person what matters most.* Nico reads the air correctly (skill) and the plastic incorrectly (limits), and loses a year of his uncle's work. Teo names it in paragraph 27.

Distractors B, C, and D are all statements *about* the story that the story does not develop. D is the strongest distractor because Nico believes it — students must distinguish a character's view from the text's theme.

---

### Item 6 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.4.a · DOK 1 · I-TEC

**D.** *alliteration.* "salt spray softens… sun"; "gull glides… grass goes gold."

This is a floor item. **A student who misses item 6 has a vocabulary gap in poetic terminology, not a comprehension problem** — teach the term, do not reteach the poem.

---

### Item 7 — Key: **Part A = A · Part B = B** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.4.a · DOK 3 · I-TEC

**Part A — A.** "The small dark cross against the glare" reduces the flier to a tiny silhouette, conveying the ground-level speaker's distance and helplessness.

**Part B — B.** A single-line stanza after three long stanzas isolates the plea. The repetition ("Come down. Come down.") and the shift from instruction to appeal land with more force because nothing surrounds the line.

---

### Item 8 — Key: **A and C** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.1.c · DOK 3 · I-TEC

**A.** Both texts contain a warning from an experienced maker about height (Teo, ¶8; the speaker, lines 7–12).
**C.** Both show care through making: Teo prints the frame over a year; the speaker strings the wings from "all the hours I never said / I spent on them."

| Distractor | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| B | Neither modern text includes a god or a punishment — this is the *myth*, not the adaptations. Choosing B indicates a student is merging the myth box with the modern texts. |
| D | True of the story (first person, Nico) but false of the poem (the *maker* speaks). |
| E | Neither setting is hot and crowded. |

---

### Item 9 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.T.PM.1.a · DOK 2 · I-PRA

**A.** The maker-warns/flier-ignores structure is the element carried across. B, C, and D are all elements the modern story deliberately *drops* — which is exactly what item 10 asks about.

---

### Item 10 — Key: **Part A = C · Part B = B** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.1.d, 7.T.PM.1.a · DOK 3 · I-PRA

**Part A — C.** Icarus drowns; Nico survives and loses only the drone.

**Part B — B.** Because the flier lives, the story's ending can be instructional rather than punitive: Teo's last lines reframe the failure as a diagnosable mistake ("You read the plastic wrong… it is the one I keep making too") and the two walk back to the shop.

This is the **Distinguished-level** item on the form. Expect 25%–35% of students to earn both points at pre-test.

# PART 2 — Items 11–24

---

### Item 11 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.T.SS.1.a · DOK 2 · I-CSS

**C.** *problem and its solutions.* "Turning It Around" opens by naming what makes light pollution unusual (it stops when you stop it) and then lists four remedies plus a case example. The *why* half of the answer matters: the structure exists to show the losses in "What We Lose" are reversible.

---

### Item 12 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.2.a · DOK 2 · I-TEC

**B.** The section's controlling idea covers turtles, songbirds, insects, *and* humans. Distractor A names only the first example; C names only the last. Both are **part-for-whole errors** — the single most common main-idea error at this grade. If several students choose A or C, teach "the main idea must cover every paragraph in the section."

---

### Item 13 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.2.a · DOK 2 · I-TEC

**D.** Reading down the table: 17.8 → 15 stars; 19.4 → 90; 20.6 → 210; 21.9 → 2,300. As the measurement rises, visible stars rise.

| Distractor | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| A | Merrick (coastal, 20.6) is darker than Atlanta and Macon but not as dark as the state park (21.9). |
| B | 17.8 is not half of 21.9, and the scale is not linear. |
| C | The table shows a single year; it contains no 1925 comparison. |

---

### Item 14 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.L.V.2.a · DOK 1 · I-LNG

**A.** *giving off light.* The root is supplied in the stem; the context ("a **luminous** dome that can be detected from more than a hundred miles away") confirms it.

---

### Item 15 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.L.V.2.b · DOK 2 · I-LNG

**C.** *to block or shield something.* Distractors A, B, and D are all real meanings of *screen*; only C works as a verb in a sentence whose purpose clause is "so that light points down instead of sideways." **B is the diagnostic distractor** — it is a *noun* meaning. A student choosing B has not used part of speech to narrow meaning, which is precisely what 7.L.V.2.b requires.

---

### Item 16 — Key: **Part A = B · Part B = B** · 2 pts · 7.T.C.1.a · DOK 3 · I-CSS

**Part A — B.** The final section and closing line ("one of the easiest things to get back") argue that ordinary changes work.
**Part B — B.** That persuasive purpose targets the people who decide about lighting — councils, planners, property owners — which is why the article names ordinances, Tucson, and new construction.

---

### Item 17 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.3.a · DOK 2 · I-TEC

**B.** Paragraph 2: "It should adopt a dark-sky ordinance, and it should do it now." Paragraph 8 restates it as a call to action.

Distractor **A** uses the *hook* (the nine floodlights) as the claim. Students who choose A are treating the opening anecdote as the argument — a recurring error worth one direct mini-lesson.

---

### Item 18 — Key: **Part A = B · Part B = B** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.3.a · DOK 3 · I-TEC

**Part A — B.** ¶3: "Some residents are firm in their view that brighter is safer."
**Part B — B.** The Tucson evidence answers *that specific* counterclaim. A and D are real evidence in the editorial but answer different points (wildlife cost, city cost); C is not evidence at all.

Part B is a **relevance** test, not a recall test. A student who gets Part A but misses Part B can find a counterclaim but cannot yet trace which evidence answers it — that is a Developing-level profile on argumentative technique.

---

### Item 19 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.L.V.3.c · DOK 2 · I-LNG

**C.** *rigid.* All four options share the denotation "not easily moved from a position." *Steady*, *certain*, and *confident* carry neutral-to-positive connotations; *rigid* carries the negative shade of inflexibility.

---

### Item 20 — Key: **Part A = B · Part B = A** · 2 pts · 7.T.C.2.a, 7.T.C.2.b · DOK 3 · I-CSS

**Part A — B.** The editorial holds both that the loss is real and that it is reversible, and urges action now.
**Part B — A.** Whitcomb states the opposing view in its strongest form ("I understand the instinct") before answering it with evidence. Fair representation of an opposing view raises a writer's credibility.

Distractor **C** is worth discussing in class: students often assume that strong feeling *increases* credibility. It usually does the opposite in argumentative writing, and the editorial deliberately avoids it.

---

### Item 21 — Key: **Part A = C · Part B = B** · 2 pts · 7.L.GC.2.a · DOK 2 · I-LNG

**Part A — C.** *Because…* introduces a subordinate clause giving the reason the main clause is true.
**Part B — B.** Placing the reason first answers the cost objection before the reader can raise it.

This item assesses syntax **in reading** (7.L.GC.2.a), not in writing. Item 32 and 33 assess syntax in writing.

---

### Item 22 — Key: **a = S1 · b = S2 · c = B · d = B** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.2.b · DOK 3 · I-TEC

| Cell | Key | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| a. numerical measurements in a table | **S1** | Table 1 appears only in the article |
| b. personal memory | **S2** | ¶6: the Milky Way over Marsh Street; his daughter |
| c. Tucson's shielded lighting | **B** | S1: cut skyglow 7%, lowered energy bill. S2: crime rate did not rise. **Same case, different evidence emphasized** — this is the heart of 7.T.T.2.b. |
| d. reversible quickly | **B** | S1: "it stops the moment you stop it." S2: "reversed by the end of a single evening." |

Scoring: 2 = all four; 1 = exactly three; 0 = two or fewer.

---

### Item 23 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.T.RA.2.b · DOK 2 · I-PRA

**A.** The peer-reviewed-style science article reports researchers' findings on melatonin — credible *and* relevant to the health question. B is credible on local policy but is an opinion piece and says nothing about health; C has a commercial interest; D is a single anecdote.

Students must weigh **both** credibility and relevance. A student who picks B has judged relevance to the *topic* but not to the *question*.

---

### Item 24 — Key: **Part A = B · Part B = C** · 2 pts · 7.T.RA.2.a · DOK 2 · I-PRA

**Part A — B.** Only the Tucson evidence speaks to *both* halves of the question (reduced light pollution AND no loss of safety).
**Part B — C.** Entry 3 gives author, title, website name, and access date, but no publication date.

# PART 3 — Peer Revision Task, Items 25–38

---

### Item 25 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.T.SS.1.d · Trait 1 · DOK 2 · C-CSS

**D** states the claim *and* previews the three reasons the draft actually develops (wildlife, cost, fairness to existing property). A is off-topic; B repeats sentence 2; C is a personal aside inappropriate to the audience.

---

### Item 26 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.3.c · Trait 2 · DOK 2 · C-TEC

**B** is a reason (a claim that can be supported) and it is supported by both sources ("the human eye sees better under even, dim light"; "Glare is not safety"). A and D are facts unrelated to the claim; C undercuts the writer's own Tucson evidence.

---

### Item 27 — Key: **A and D** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.3.c, 7.T.RA.2.c · Trait 2 · DOK 3 · C-TEC

The reason in ¶2 is wildlife harm. **A** (UGA, 30% of hatchlings misdirected — Source 1) and **D** (41 disoriented nests locally — Source 2) both support it and come from *different* sources, which is what the item requires.

**B** is from the right source but supports the wrong reason (sky brightening, not wildlife). **C** is from the right source but is a personal memory, not evidence.

Scoring: 2 = A and D only; 1 = exactly one of A/D plus at most one incorrect; 0 = otherwise.

**This item is the single best predictor of Trait 2 performance on the essay.** Students who cannot select cross-source evidence in a multiple-choice format will not integrate it in composition.

---

### Item 28 — Key: **Part A = C · Part B = B** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.2.d · Trait 2 · DOK 2 · C-TEC

**Part A — C.** The paragraph already has evidence (S12, Tucson) but no explanation of *why* it supports the money reason. C supplies the causal link.
**Part B — B.** Elaboration explains how evidence connects to the point; it does not add new reasons (A), cite (C), or transition (D).

Distractor **D in Part A** ($214,000) is a strong distractor because it is *good evidence* — but the paragraph does not need more evidence, it needs explanation. Distinguishing evidence from elaboration is the central Trait 2 skill and is worth an entire mini-lesson.

---

### Item 29 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.3.c · Trait 2 · DOK 2 · C-TEC

**A** names the opposing view *and* signals that it will be answered — which is exactly what ¶4 goes on to do. B is filler; C dismisses rather than addresses; D concedes the opposing point without answering it.

---

### Item 30 — Key: **Part A = D · Part B = A** · 2 pts · 7.T.SS.1.c · Trait 1 · DOK 3 · C-CSS

**Part A — D.** "Beyond its effects on wildlife," closes the paragraph just completed and opens the next reason, creating cohesion *between* paragraphs.
**Part B — A.** "Just as importantly," signals a coordinate reason of equal weight, which is the actual relationship between the cost reason and the fairness reason.

Distractors test whether students read the *relationship* rather than reaching for any transition: "For example" signals illustration; "However" signals contrast; "As a result" signals causation. None of those relationships exists here.

---

### Item 31 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.T.SS.2.c · Trait 1 · DOK 2 · C-CSS

**B.** Sentence 11 — "Basically, the city would be throwing away way less cash on lighting up the clouds" — uses *Basically*, *way less*, and *cash*: a conversational register inappropriate for a city council.

---

### Item 32 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.L.GC.2.b · Trait 3 · DOK 2 · C-LNG

**C.** Sentence 14 shifts to past tense (*covered*, *changed*) inside a present-tense argument that also contains a present-tense verb (*is already standing*). Both verbs must move to present.

Distractor **D (no change needed)** is the diagnostic response: a student choosing D does not hear the shift at all and needs work at the sentence level before revision work will help.

---

### Item 33 — Key: **A and D** · 2 pts · 7.L.GC.2.c · Trait 3 · DOK 2 · C-LNG

**A.** S6 — "Sea turtle hatchlings **are misdirected by** beachfront lighting" → *Beachfront lighting misdirects sea turtle hatchlings.*
**D.** S18 — "These standards **have been adopted by** more than twenty communities" → *More than twenty communities have adopted these standards.*

Both contain the *be* + past participle + *by*-phrase signature. B, C, and E are active.

Scoring: 2 = A and D only; 1 = exactly one correct with at most one incorrect; 0 = otherwise, including three or more marks.

---

### Item 34 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.L.GC.2.d · Trait 3 · DOK 2 · C-LNG

**D** attaches "point down instead of sideways" to *fixtures*, the only noun it can logically modify.

**A** appears to fix the modifier but still leaves it modifying *new buildings* (buildings do not point down) **and** converts the sentence to passive voice, which 7.L.GC.2.c asks students to avoid. **This is the highest-quality distractor on the form** — it rewards students who track both standards at once.

---

### Item 35 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.L.GC.1 (GUM) · Trait 3 · DOK 1 · C-LNG

**A.** Sentence 17 lists three items that each already contain an internal comma, so the items must be separated by semicolons:

> The ordinance would require three things: fixtures that point down, not sideways; bulbs that are warm, not blue; and timers that shut lights off, not leave them burning.

This skill is marked **M (Master) at Grade 6** on the GUM chart and is therefore reinforced, not introduced, in Grade 7.

---

### Item 36 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.L.V.2.d · Trait 3 · DOK 2 · C-LNG

**B.** *approach* names what "this" actually refers to: the set of lighting standards. *Thing*, *deal*, and *business* are equally vague or add an inappropriate register.

---

### Item 37 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.T.RA.2.c · Trait 2 · DOK 2 · C-TEC

**C** identifies the author, the publication, and the borrowed information — a signal phrase, the standard classroom form. A is a malformed parenthetical; B is plagiarism disguised as common knowledge; D adds an assertion of truth but still credits no one.

---

### Item 38 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.T.SS.1.d · Trait 1 · DOK 2 · C-CSS

**D** finalizes the argument: it returns to the decision point, states the stakes, and closes with the cost-of-action image. A opens a new topic; B widens the scope at the moment the argument should close; C addresses the teacher rather than the council.

# PART 4 — Item 39: Extended Writing Task

Score with the Georgia Milestones Grade 7 Eight-Point, Three-Trait Writing Rubric. Full scoring procedure, anchor descriptors, condition codes, and the double-scoring protocol are in **`rubrics/Writing-Rubric-and-Scoring-Guide.md`**.

**Record three separate trait scores in the reporting workbook** — not a single total. The workbook routes them to three different reporting categories:

| Field | Range | Routes to |
|---|---|---|
| `Q39_T1` — Purpose & Organization | 0–3 | Constructing: Context + Structure & Style |
| `Q39_T2` — Evidence & Elaboration | 0–3 | Constructing: Techniques |
| `Q39_T3` — Language Usage & Conventions | 0–2 | Constructing: Language |

**Form A prompt-specific notes for scorers:**

- **Evidence expected from Source 1 (Achebe):** the 9.6% annual brightening figure; Table 1 measurements; the UGA hatchling estimate; the melatonin finding; the four practical remedies; Tucson's 7% skyglow reduction and lower energy bill.
- **Evidence expected from Source 2 (Whitcomb):** Tucson's flat crime rate; the 41 disoriented nests; the $214,000 electricity figure; the point that an ordinance touches only new construction.
- **A response using only one source cannot earn 3 on Trait 2.** The trait explicitly requires "evidence from multiple sources." Cap at 2.
- **Either position is fully creditable.** A well-argued case *against* the ordinance — cost, enforcement burden, local control — can earn 3/3/2. Score the argument, never the conclusion.
- **Credible counterclaims available to students:** the safety concern (¶3 of Source 2); cost of compliance; the fact that an ordinance on new construction does nothing about existing lighting.
