# FORM B (POST-TEST) — Answer Key, Rationales & Scoring Rules

**SECURE DOCUMENT — do not distribute to students.**

Form B is the item-for-item isomorph of Form A. Item *n* on this form assesses the same expectation, at the same DOK, in the same item type, for the same points as item *n* on Form A. Only the passage content and the answer positions differ.

---

## Scoring rules for multi-point items

Identical to Form A. Apply exactly the same way, or the pre/post comparison is compromised.

| Item type | 2 points | 1 point | 0 points |
|---|---|---|---|
| **EBSR / Multiple-part** (Part A + Part B) | Both parts correct | Exactly one part correct | Neither part correct |
| **Multiple-select** ("Select TWO") | Both correct choices marked, no incorrect choices marked | Exactly one correct choice marked and no more than one incorrect choice marked | Any other response, **including marking three or more choices** |
| **Match** (item 22, four cells) | All four cells correct | Exactly three cells correct | Two or fewer cells correct |
| **Extended writing** (item 39) | See `rubrics/Writing-Rubric-and-Scoring-Guide.md` | | |

# PART 1 — Items 1–10

---

### Item 1 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.1.a · DOK 2 · I-TEC

**A.** Talia answers a direct instruction ("Work it slow") with "Slow is thin" and then "I didn't put a year of your life into this loom so I could take another red ribbon" — she treats the warning as an obstacle to winning. Distractor B confuses rejecting *advice* with distrusting *skill*; Talia calls the warp "a good warp."

---

### Item 2 — Key: **Part A = C · Part B = D** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.1.b · DOK 3 · I-TEC

**Part A — C, flashback.** "I remember the afternoon she first showed me the draft on paper. I was eleven."
**Part B — D.** *"Then it does not hold. It remembers. It lets go all at once."* — the exact failure mechanism of the warp in ¶18–19.

*Note:* Part A distractor **A (foreshadowing)** is the common error. The paragraph contains foreshadowing but is *structured* as flashback.

---

### Item 3 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.L.V.3.b · DOK 2 · I-LNG

**C.** *a force that pulls or stretches something tight.* The clue lies in the following paragraph: "It came up through the warp in a long even pull… Tension. A whole field of it, gathering."

Distractor **D** (*an injury from working a muscle*) is the most common; it is a real meaning defeated only by context.

---

### Item 4 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.T.SS.2.a · DOK 2 · I-CSS

**A.** *tense, confining.* "Cage," "narrow," "bars," and "nothing between the bars at all" carry confinement and emptiness. The metaphor immediately precedes the warning scene.

---

### Item 5 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.1.c · DOK 2 · I-TEC

**B.** Talia reads the tension correctly (skill) and the thread incorrectly (limits), and loses a year of her teacher's work. Mrs. Whitfield names it in ¶27. Distractor D is what Talia *believes*; students must separate a character's view from the text's theme.

---

### Item 6 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.4.a · DOK 1 · I-TEC

**A.** *alliteration.* "slow shed settles… sett"; "shuttle sings… selvage."

A student who misses this has a terminology gap, not a comprehension problem.

---

### Item 7 — Key: **Part A = B · Part B = C** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.4.a · DOK 3 · I-TEC

**Part A — B.** "The small quick shuttle against the shade" reduces the work to a tiny moving silhouette seen from behind — the speaker's vantage point.
**Part B — C.** The single-line stanza isolates the plea after three long stanzas; the repetition ("Slow down. Slow down.") and the shift from instruction to appeal land harder with nothing around them.

---

### Item 8 — Key: **A and C** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.1.c · DOK 3 · I-TEC

**A.** Both texts contain a warning about hurrying (Mrs. Whitfield, ¶8; the speaker, lines 7–12).
**C.** Both show care through making: the warped loom over a year; "all the winters I never said / I spent on it."

**B** belongs to the *myth*, not the adaptations — choosing it indicates a student is merging the myth box with the modern texts. **D** is true of the story but false of the poem (the *maker* speaks). **E** is false of both.

---

### Item 9 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.T.PM.1.a · DOK 2 · I-PRA

**C.** The master-warns/young-weaver-ignores structure is what carries across. A, B, and D are the elements the modern story deliberately drops.

---

### Item 10 — Key: **Part A = C · Part B = C** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.1.d, 7.T.PM.1.a · DOK 3 · I-PRA

**Part A — C.** Athena destroys Arachne's work; Talia's weaving fails because *she* pushed the beater. Agency moves from the goddess to the girl.
**Part B — C.** Because the failure is Talia's own, the ending can be instructional rather than punitive: Mrs. Whitfield reframes it as a diagnosable mistake ("You read the thread wrong… it is the one I keep making too").

This is the **Distinguished-level** item on the form. Expect 25%–35% of students to earn both points.

# PART 2 — Items 11–24

---

### Item 11 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.T.SS.1.a · DOK 2 · I-CSS

**D.** *causes and effects.* The section is built on three parallel "When X… so Y" constructions (glued battery → whole device retired; sealed case → four-dollar repair ends a four-hundred-dollar machine; updates stop → working machine discarded), followed by "Reverse those decisions and the effects reverse with them."

---

### Item 12 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.2.a · DOK 2 · I-TEC

**A.** The section covers battery fires, lead and flame retardants in groundwater, *and* dioxins from open burning. Distractors B and C each name only one part of the section — the **part-for-whole error**, the most common main-idea failure at this grade.

---

### Item 13 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.2.a · DOK 2 · I-TEC

**B.** Small appliances lead in units retired (205 million) and trail in recycling rate (9%). This requires reading *two columns against each other*, which is the Grade 7 demand.

| Distractor | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| A | Smartphones are recycled at 17%, the second-lowest rate. |
| C | Laptops and tablets are at 24%, not over 50%. |
| D | The table shows a single year; no trend data appears. |

---

### Item 14 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.L.V.2.a · DOK 1 · I-LNG

**D.** *a small piece broken off from something larger.* The root is supplied; the context ("every **fragment** of that material is lost from the supply") confirms it.

---

### Item 15 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.L.V.2.b · DOK 2 · I-LNG

**B.** *to flow steadily in a continuous current.* **A is the diagnostic distractor** — it is the meaning students meet daily, but it does not work as a verb whose subject is *flame retardants* and whose object is *groundwater*. C and D are noun meanings. A student choosing C or D has not used part of speech to narrow meaning.

---

### Item 16 — Key: **Part A = C · Part B = B** · 2 pts · 7.T.C.1.a · DOK 3 · I-CSS

**Part A — C.** The final section and closing lines ("What they specify, manufacturers build"; "design choices can be made differently") argue that purchasing can change outcomes.
**Part B — B.** That purpose targets large-scale purchasers — which is why the article names school districts explicitly.

---

### Item 17 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.3.a · DOK 2 · I-TEC

**D.** ¶2: "It should require that the devices we buy be repairable, and it should do it now." ¶9 restates it as a call to action.

Distractor **A** uses the *hook* (the forty-one Chromebooks) as the claim — the recurring hook-as-claim error.

---

### Item 18 — Key: **Part A = B · Part B = C** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.3.a · DOK 3 · I-TEC

**Part A — B.** ¶3: "Some board members hold that the cheapest device is the responsible device."
**Part B — C.** The forty-dollar / twice-as-long comparison directly answers the cost counterclaim. A and D are real evidence answering different points; B is not evidence.

A student who gets Part A but misses Part B can locate a counterclaim but cannot yet trace which evidence answers it — a Developing-level profile on argumentative technique.

---

### Item 19 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.L.V.3.c · DOK 2 · I-LNG

**A.** *stingy.* All four options share the denotation "unwilling to spend much money." *Thrifty*, *economical*, and *practical* are neutral to positive; *stingy* is the negative shade.

---

### Item 20 — Key: **Part A = C · Part B = C** · 2 pts · 7.T.C.2.a, 7.T.C.2.b · DOK 3 · I-CSS

**Part A — C.** The editorial holds that the cost is real, that it is avoidable, and that the district should act at the October contract.
**Part B — C.** Boateng states the opposing view in its strongest form ("I understand the instinct") before answering it with numbers. Fair representation raises credibility.

Distractor **B** is worth class discussion: students often assume strong feeling increases credibility. It usually does the opposite.

---

### Item 21 — Key: **Part A = B · Part B = B** · 2 pts · 7.L.GC.2.a · DOK 2 · I-LNG

**Part A — B.** *Although…* introduces a subordinate clause conceding a point that appears to work against the main idea.
**Part B — B.** Placing the concession first disarms the cost objection before the main clause answers it.

Assesses syntax **in reading**; items 32–34 assess syntax in writing.

---

### Item 22 — Key: **a = S1 · b = S2 · c = B · d = B** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.2.b · DOK 3 · I-TEC

| Cell | Key | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| a. numerical measurements in a table | **S1** | Table 1 appears only in the article |
| b. personal memory | **S2** | ¶7: the projector, the work order, the screwdriver |
| c. how much longer a repairable device stays in service | **B** | S1: "nearly twice as long as a sealed one." S2: "it stays in service nearly twice as long." **Same finding, framed as design science in S1 and as budget math in S2** — the heart of 7.T.T.2.b. |
| d. reduced through purchasing decisions | **B** | S1: "What they specify, manufacturers build." S2: "Vote for the repair standard in October." |

Scoring: 2 = all four; 1 = exactly three; 0 = two or fewer.

---

### Item 23 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.T.RA.2.b · DOK 2 · I-PRA

**B.** The science article reports national recycling data — credible *and* relevant to the question. A is credible on local policy but is an opinion piece with no recycling-rate data; C has a commercial interest; D is a single anecdote.

A student who picks A has judged relevance to the *topic* but not to the *question*.

---

### Item 24 — Key: **Part A = C · Part B = C** · 2 pts · 7.T.RA.2.a · DOK 2 · I-PRA

**Part A — C.** Only the forty-dollar / twice-as-long evidence speaks to *both* halves of the question (less waste AND no cost increase).
**Part B — C.** Entry 3 gives author, title, website name, and access date, but no publication date.

# PART 3 — Peer Revision Task, Items 25–38

---

### Item 25 — Key: **A** · 1 pt · 7.T.SS.1.d · Trait 1 · DOK 2 · C-CSS

**A** states the claim *and* previews the three reasons the draft develops (waste, six-year cost, no effect on existing machines). B is off-topic; C repeats sentence 2; D is a personal aside inappropriate to a school board.

---

### Item 26 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.3.c · Trait 2 · DOK 2 · C-TEC

**D** is a reason that can be supported and that the sources support (a case that opens is a case a class can look inside; "Most of them have never opened anything they own"). A and C are facts unrelated to the claim; B undercuts the writer's own Southeast-districts evidence.

---

### Item 27 — Key: **A and D** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.3.c, 7.T.RA.2.c · Trait 2 · DOK 3 · C-TEC

The reason in ¶2 is environmental harm. **A** (5,000 battery fires a year — Source 1) and **D** (14 tons at the county site — Source 2) both support it and come from *different* sources.

**B** is from the right source but supports a different point (household storage, not harm). **C** is from the right source but is a personal memory, not evidence.

Scoring: 2 = A and D only; 1 = exactly one of A/D plus at most one incorrect; 0 = otherwise.

**This item is the single best predictor of Trait 2 performance on the essay.**

---

### Item 28 — Key: **Part A = D · Part B = C** · 2 pts · 7.T.T.2.d · Trait 2 · DOK 2 · C-TEC

**Part A — D.** ¶3 already has evidence (S12) but no explanation of *why* it supports the money reason. D supplies the causal link.
**Part B — C.** Elaboration explains how evidence connects to the point; it does not credit (A), add reasons (B), or transition (D).

Distractor **C in Part A** (the 14-ton figure) is strong because it is *good evidence* — but the paragraph needs explanation, not more evidence. Distinguishing evidence from elaboration is the central Trait 2 skill.

---

### Item 29 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.T.T.3.c · Trait 2 · DOK 2 · C-TEC

**C** names the opposing view *and* signals it will be answered, which is what ¶4 goes on to do. A is filler; B dismisses rather than addresses; D concedes without answering.

---

### Item 30 — Key: **Part A = B · Part B = C** · 2 pts · 7.T.SS.1.c · Trait 1 · DOK 3 · C-CSS

**Part A — B.** "Beyond its effects on the environment," closes the completed paragraph and opens the next reason, creating cohesion *between* paragraphs.
**Part B — C.** "Just as importantly," signals a coordinate reason of equal weight — the actual relationship between the cost reason and the fairness reason.

Distractors test whether students read the *relationship*: "For example" signals illustration, "However" contrast, "As a result" causation. None of those relationships exists here.

---

### Item 31 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.T.SS.2.c · Trait 1 · DOK 2 · C-CSS

**D.** Sentence 11 — "Basically, the district would be throwing away way less cash on brand new machines" — uses *Basically*, *way less*, and *cash*: a conversational register inappropriate for a school board.

---

### Item 32 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.L.GC.2.b · Trait 3 · DOK 2 · C-LNG

**B.** Sentence 14 shifts to past tense (*applied*, *changed*) inside a present-tense argument that also contains a present-tense verb (*is already in our closets*). Both verbs must move to present.

Distractor **D (no change needed)** is diagnostic: a student choosing D does not hear the shift and needs sentence-level work before revision work will help.

---

### Item 33 — Key: **B and D** · 2 pts · 7.L.GC.2.c · Trait 3 · DOK 2 · C-LNG

**B.** S6 — "Lithium-ion batteries **are crushed by** compactors" → *Compactors crush lithium-ion batteries.*
**D.** S18 — "These specifications **have been adopted by** several large districts" → *Several large districts have adopted these specifications.*

Both show the *be* + past participle + *by*-phrase signature. A, C, and E are active.

Scoring: 2 = B and D only; 1 = exactly one correct with at most one incorrect; 0 = otherwise, including three or more marks.

---

### Item 34 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.L.GC.2.d · Trait 3 · DOK 2 · C-LNG

**C** attaches "clip in instead of gluing in" to *batteries*, the only noun it can logically modify.

**A** appears to fix the modifier but still leaves it modifying *new devices* **and** converts the sentence to passive voice, which 7.L.GC.2.c asks students to avoid. This is the highest-quality distractor on the form.

---

### Item 35 — Key: **D** · 1 pt · 7.L.GC.1 (GUM) · Trait 3 · DOK 1 · C-LNG

**D.** Sentence 17 lists three items that each already contain an internal comma, so the items must be separated by semicolons:

> The standard would require three things: cases that open with ordinary tools, not proprietary screws; batteries that clip in, not glue in; and software updates that continue for six years, not three.

Marked **M (Master) at Grade 6** on the GUM chart — reinforced, not introduced, in Grade 7.

---

### Item 36 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.L.V.2.d · Trait 3 · DOK 2 · C-LNG

**C.** *specifications* names what "these" actually refers to: the three purchasing requirements listed in S17. *Deals*, *matters*, and *situations* are equally vague or add an inappropriate register.

---

### Item 37 — Key: **B** · 1 pt · 7.T.RA.2.c · Trait 2 · DOK 2 · C-TEC

**B** identifies the author, the publication, and the borrowed information. A is a malformed parenthetical; C is plagiarism disguised as common knowledge; D asserts truth but credits no one.

---

### Item 38 — Key: **C** · 1 pt · 7.T.SS.1.d · Trait 1 · DOK 2 · C-CSS

**C** finalizes the argument: it returns to the decision point, states the stakes, and closes with the cost-of-action image. A opens a new topic; B widens the scope at the moment the argument should close; D addresses the teacher rather than the board.

# PART 4 — Item 39: Extended Writing Task

Score with the Georgia Milestones Grade 7 Eight-Point, Three-Trait Writing Rubric. **Use the same procedure, the same anchors, and the same scorers as at pre-test** — a change in scoring severity between September and April will masquerade as student growth.

**Record three separate trait scores:**

| Field | Range | Routes to |
|---|---|---|
| `Q39_T1` — Purpose & Organization | 0–3 | Constructing: Context + Structure & Style |
| `Q39_T2` — Evidence & Elaboration | 0–3 | Constructing: Techniques |
| `Q39_T3` — Language Usage & Conventions | 0–2 | Constructing: Language |

**Form B prompt-specific notes for scorers:**

- **Evidence expected from Source 1 (Vanterpool):** the 62-million-ton figure; the 22% formal recycling rate; Table 1 figures; the sixty elements in a smartphone; the 5,000 battery fires; the twice-as-long service life; repairability scores; districts as major purchasers.
- **Evidence expected from Source 2 (Boateng):** the forty-one Chromebooks and the thirty-eight needing one small part; the forty-dollar / twice-as-long comparison; the 14 tons at the county drop-off site; the point that a standard touches only new purchases.
- **A response using only one source cannot earn 3 on Trait 2.** Cap at 2.
- **Either position is fully creditable.** A well-argued case *against* a repair standard — up-front cost, vendor availability, warranty risk — can earn 3/3/2. Score the argument, never the conclusion.
- **Credible counterclaims available to students:** the up-front cost objection (¶3); the fact that a standard does nothing about devices already owned; the concern that repairable machines may be bulkier or less available.
