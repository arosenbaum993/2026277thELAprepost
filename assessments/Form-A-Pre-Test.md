## Passage 1: Wax Wings

*a short story by Delia Marchetti*

**1**  The first thing Uncle Teo ever taught me about flying was that the air is not empty.

**2**  "People look up and see nothing," he said, the morning he handed me my first controller. We were standing in the gravel lot behind his shop in Merrick, and the marsh grass beyond the fence was bent flat by wind off the sound. "But air has rivers in it. Cold rivers, warm rivers. You do not fight them. You read them."

**3**  I was nine then. I nodded like I understood. I did not.

**4**  Four years later I understood enough to win. The Coastal Circuit finals were held on the county airstrip, and I had taken second in the qualifying heat — close enough behind Dev Ramachandran that I could see the reflection of his drone in the puddles when he passed me. Second is a good place to be. Second means you know exactly how much faster you have to go.

**5**  Teo spent the whole night before the final in the shop with the door open, the fluorescent light spilling out onto the gravel. He was printing a new frame for me, lighter than the old one by forty grams, the arms swept back like a swift's wings. He had been working on the design for a year. He would not let me touch it until it was finished.

**6**  I remember the night he first showed me the drawings. I was eleven. He had spread them across the workbench under a lamp, and I had leaned in so close that my breath fogged the paper. The frame was beautiful. It was also thin — so thin in places that the plastic went translucent when you held it up to the light. "Printed plastic is strong until it is warm," Teo said that night, tapping the narrowest arm. "Then it is not strong. It is patient. It waits." I laughed, because I was eleven and everything my uncle said sounded like a riddle. He did not laugh.

**7**  In the morning the frame was on the bench, matte black and finished, and the sky over the airstrip was a sheet of cold glass — no cloud, no give, nothing in it at all.

**8**  "Fly it low," Teo said. He was not looking at me. He was looking up. "The heat comes off that runway after ten o'clock. It will lift you if you let it. Stay under thirty meters and let Dev burn himself out up there."

**9**  "Low is slow," I said.

**10**  "Low is finishing."

**11**  "Second place is finishing." I clipped the battery in and felt the frame flex a little, the way a good frame should. "I didn't build a year of your life into a drone so I could come in second, Tío."

**12**  He did not answer that. I have thought about the silence a lot since.

**13**  The horn sounded at ten-forty. For two laps I did exactly what he said. I ran the course low, so low the grass whipped underneath me, and I held third while Dev and a girl from Brunswick traded the lead above.

**14**  Then, on the third lap, I saw the **drift**.

**15**  It came off the black runway in a long invisible column, and I could read it the way Teo had taught me — by the way the windsock stuttered, by the way a gull crossing above the tarmac rose without moving its wings. Warm air. A river of it, going up. If I climbed into that column at the right moment, it would carry me over the back straight faster than any motor could push me.

**16**  I climbed.

**17**  It worked. That is the part nobody believes. For eleven seconds it worked perfectly. The warm air took the swept-back arms and pushed, and I came over the top of the field so far ahead of Dev that I laughed out loud into my headset — and somewhere behind me at the pilot station I heard my uncle say my name once, quietly, the way you say a word you have already given up on.

**18**  On the twelfth second the frame let go.

**19**  It did not break the way things break in movies. There was no crack. One arm simply stopped being straight. It curved, gently, like something relaxing, and then the drone was not a drone anymore. It was four hundred grams of plastic and lithium going one direction while its motors argued about another.

**20**  I watched it **drift** out over the fence line, over the ditch, over the pale gold edge of the marsh, and drop into the water of the sound without a sound I could hear.

**21**  Dev finished first. The girl from Brunswick finished second. I finished nothing.

**22**  Teo walked out to the fence with me afterward. The tide was going out. Somewhere under the brown water was a year of his evenings.

**23**  I waited for him to say it. *I told you to stay low.* He had earned it. I would have said it.

**24**  Instead he stood there a while with his hands in his pockets, looking at the marsh the way he looked at the sky.

**25**  "You read it right," he said finally.

**26**  "I lost."

**27**  "You read the *air* right, Nico." He turned. "You read the *plastic* wrong. That is a different mistake, and it is the one I keep making too." He almost smiled. "Come on. The printer is still warm."

**28**  We walked back to the shop. Behind us the sound closed over the wreck and went on shining — flat and bright and empty — and I understood at last that the air is not empty, and neither is the water, and that the only thing that had ever been empty was the space between what I could do and what I thought I could.

## Source 1: The Vanishing Night

*by Rosalind Achebe · Georgia Science Review, March 2024*

### A Sky That Used to Be Dark

If you had stood in a Georgia field in 1925 and looked up, you would have seen roughly 2,500 stars. Stand in the same field tonight, in most parts of the state, and you will see fewer than 200. Nothing has happened to the stars. Something has happened to the night.

The cause is artificial light at night, often shortened to ALAN — the glow thrown upward by streetlights, parking lots, billboards, stadiums, and porch fixtures. Seen from a distance, a modern city sits beneath a **luminous** dome that can be detected from more than a hundred miles away. A 2023 satellite study found that the world's night sky is growing brighter by about 9.6 percent each year. At that rate, a child born under a sky showing 250 stars will, by the time that child turns eighteen, see about 100.

Astronomers measure this brightening in magnitudes per square arcsecond, a scale on which *smaller* numbers mean a *brighter* sky.

**Table 1. Night-sky brightness at four Georgia locations, 2023**

| Location | Sky brightness (mag/arcsec²) | Stars visible to the unaided eye |
|---|---:|---:|
| Downtown Atlanta | 17.8 | 15 |
| Suburban Macon | 19.4 | 90 |
| Merrick (coastal) | 20.6 | 210 |
| Stephen C. Foster State Park | 21.9 | 2,300 |

### What We Lose

The cost of a bright night is not only a lost view. Light at night reshapes the behavior of living things that spent millions of years evolving under darkness.

Sea turtle hatchlings on Georgia's barrier islands find the ocean by moving toward the brightest horizon, which for all of history was the open water reflecting the sky. Beachfront lighting reverses that signal. Researchers at the University of Georgia estimate that unshielded lighting near nesting beaches misdirects up to 30 percent of hatchlings, sending them inland toward roads.

Migrating songbirds, most of which travel at night, navigate partly by the stars. Bright buildings pull them off course and hold them circling until they are exhausted. Insects — including the pollinators that farms depend on — gather at lights and die there. And human beings are not exempt: light entering the eye at night suppresses melatonin, the hormone that governs sleep. Sleep researchers now advise that bedrooms be kept as dark as a closet.

### Turning It Around

Here is what makes light pollution unusual among environmental problems: it stops the moment you stop it. Carbon dioxide lingers in the atmosphere for centuries. A poorly aimed floodlight stops polluting the second it is aimed at the ground.

That is why the practical solutions are so ordinary. Communities that have reduced their skyglow have generally done four things. They **screen** fixtures so that light points down instead of sideways. They switch to warmer-colored bulbs, since blue-rich white light scatters farthest through the atmosphere. They install motion sensors and timers so that lights burn only when something needs to be seen. And they lower overall brightness — which, surprisingly, improves visibility, because the human eye sees better under even, dim light than under a patchwork of glare and shadow.

More than twenty communities in the United States have adopted "dark-sky" ordinances requiring shielded, warm, timed outdoor lighting on new construction. Tucson, Arizona, a city of more than half a million people, cut its skyglow by 7 percent between 2018 and 2020 while lowering its street-lighting energy bill. The city did not go dark. It simply stopped throwing light where no one was standing.

The night sky is one of the few things every human being who has ever lived has shared. It is also, at the moment, one of the easiest things to get back.

## Source 2: Turn Down the Lights, Merrick

*an editorial by Jamal Whitcomb · The Merrick Sentinel, September 8, 2024*

**1**  Last month the county installed nine new floodlights in the lot behind the community center. They are bright. From my front porch, six blocks inland, I can read the time on my watch by them.

**2**  I am not writing to complain about a parking lot. I am writing because Merrick is about to make a decision it will live with for thirty years, and almost nobody has noticed. In November the council will vote on lighting standards for all new construction inside the city limits. It should adopt a dark-sky ordinance, and it should do it now rather than after another decade of floodlights.

**3**  The argument for waiting is always the same one, and it deserves a straight answer. Some residents are **firm** in their view that brighter is safer — that a dark street is a dangerous street, and that any rule limiting light is a rule limiting safety. I understand the instinct. It is also not what the evidence shows. When Tucson, Arizona, dimmed and shielded its streetlights between 2018 and 2020, its crime rate did not rise. What improves safety is *even* light, aimed where people actually walk. Glare is not safety. Glare is a bright thing standing next to a black thing, and the human eye cannot see into the black thing at all.

**4**  Meanwhile the cost of getting this wrong is landing on our own beaches. The Georgia Sea Turtle Cooperative counted 41 disoriented nests on this island alone last summer, the highest number in its records. Those are our turtles. That is our road they are crawling toward.

**5**  Because the fixtures we already own can be replaced a few at a time, the cost of the change need not fall in a single year. An ordinance covering new construction touches nothing that is already standing. It asks only that what we build next be built pointing down. And the city has reason to want that: Merrick's own facilities report lists $214,000 in outdoor lighting electricity for last year alone.

**6**  I have lived here forty-one years. I remember when you could walk out to the end of Marsh Street in August and see the Milky Way over the sound — not a smudge, the actual band of it, bright enough to throw a shadow on the dock. My daughter is fourteen. She has never seen it. She lives in the same house I do.

**7**  That is not a nostalgic complaint. It is a measurable loss, and it is one of the only environmental losses in the world that can be reversed by the end of a single evening. Every other problem we argue about in this town takes decades to fix. This one takes a screwdriver and a decision.

**8**  Vote for the ordinance in November.
