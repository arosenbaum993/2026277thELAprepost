## Passage 1: Thread Count

*a short story by Delia Marchetti*

**1**  The first thing Mrs. Whitfield ever taught me about weaving was that the thread is not the point.

**2**  "People look at a piece and see the pattern," she said, the afternoon she handed me my first shuttle. We were sitting in the back room of her shop on Bell Street, and the ceiling fan was turning the dust in the window light. "But a weaving is tension. Warp pulling one way, weft pulling the other. You do not win against the tension. You listen to it."

**3**  I was nine then. I nodded like I understood. I did not.

**4**  Four years later I understood enough to win. The Bell County Fair textile competition was judged in the livestock barn every September, and I had taken second the year before — close enough behind Priya Raghunathan that I could see the judges hesitate before they set the ribbon down. Second is a good place to be. Second means you know exactly how much better you have to get.

**5**  Mrs. Whitfield spent the whole week before the fair with her shop door propped open, the afternoon light coming in across the floorboards. She was warping the small loom for me, a finer sett than I had ever used, sixty threads to the inch instead of forty. She had been planning the piece for a year. She would not let me sit down at it until the warp was tied.

**6**  I remember the afternoon she first showed me the draft on paper. I was eleven. She had spread it across the cutting table under the window, and I had leaned in so close that my hair fell across the grid. The pattern was beautiful. It was also tight — so tight in places that the graph-paper squares ran together into a solid gray block. "A fine sett holds until you hurry it," Mrs. Whitfield said that afternoon, tapping the densest section. "Then it does not hold. It remembers. It lets go all at once." I laughed, because I was eleven and everything she said sounded like a riddle. She did not laugh.

**7**  On the morning of the fair the loom stood finished in the corner of the barn, warped and waiting, and the sun through the high window turned the sixty threads into a cage of light — narrow, upright, nothing between the bars at all.

**8**  "Work it slow," Mrs. Whitfield said. She was not looking at me. She was looking at the warp. "That sett will beat you if you push the beater. Three hours is enough. Let Priya rush herself into a mistake."

**9**  "Slow is thin," I said.

**10**  "Slow is finished."

**11**  "Second place is finished." I set my hands on the shuttle and felt the warp give a little, the way a good warp should. "I didn't put a year of your life into this loom so I could take another red ribbon, ma'am."

**12**  She did not answer that. I have thought about the silence a lot since.

**13**  The bell rang at nine. For the first hour I did exactly what she said. I threw the shuttle slow, so slow I could hear the beater settle on every pick, and I stayed behind Priya while she built her border twice as fast as mine.

**14**  Then, in the second hour, I felt the **strain**.

**15**  It came up through the warp in a long even pull, and I could read it the way Mrs. Whitfield had taught me — by the way the selvage edges drew inward, by the way a loose thread at the far side stood up without being touched. Tension. A whole field of it, gathering. If I let the beater fall harder at the right moment, that tension would pack the weft tighter and finish the panel faster than careful hands ever could.

**16**  I pushed.

**17**  It worked. That is the part nobody believes. For most of an hour it worked perfectly. The tight warp took the harder beat and held, and the pattern came up off the loom so far ahead of Priya's that I laughed out loud over the noise of the barn — and somewhere behind me at the rail I heard Mrs. Whitfield say my name once, quietly, the way you say a word you have already given up on.

**18**  In the last ten minutes the warp let go.

**19**  It did not tear the way cloth tears in movies. There was no rip. One thread simply stopped being tight. It slackened, gently, like something relaxing, and then the panel was not a panel anymore. It was four hundred threads going one direction while the pattern argued about another.

**20**  I watched the **strain** run out across the width of the piece, past the border, past the pale gold edge of the selvage, and disappear into the bottom of the weaving without a sound I could hear.

**21**  Priya took first. A boy from Colquitt took second. I took nothing.

**22**  Mrs. Whitfield walked out to the barn door with me afterward. The light had gone flat and yellow. Somewhere in the basket at my feet was a year of her afternoons.

**23**  I waited for her to say it. *I told you to work it slow.* She had earned it. I would have said it.

**24**  Instead she stood there a while with her hands folded, looking out at the field the way she looked at a warp.

**25**  "You read it right," she said finally.

**26**  "I lost."

**27**  "You read the *tension* right, Talia." She turned. "You read the *thread* wrong. That is a different mistake, and it is the one I keep making too." She almost smiled. "Come on. The loom is still warped."

**28**  We walked back toward the shop. Behind us the barn doors stood open on all that empty light, and I understood at last that the thread is not the point, and neither is the pattern, and that the only thing that had ever been empty was the space between what I could do and what I thought I could.

## Passage 2: The Loom

*a poem by Delia Marchetti*

```
                                                    STANZA 1
 1   I strung you a loom from what I had:
 2   a borrowed reed, a beaten frame,
 3   the patient linen, the counted thread,
 4   and all the winters I never said
 5   I spent on it. A maker's math —
 6   each thread pulled tight, a thread of faith.

                                                    STANZA 2
 7   So follow, fledgling, the steady beat.
 8   The slow shed settles. The sett does not.
 9   Keep to the low count, close and cool,
10   where the shuttle sings and the selvage holds.
11   I did not warp you for the speed.
12   I warped you for the coming through.

                                                    STANZA 3
13   But you will push. I know the look:
14   the chin tipped down, the beater full,
15   the whole bright morning like a book
16   that you have decided you can read.
17   And I will stand behind you and watch
18   the small quick shuttle against the shade
19   go fast, and fast, and fray.

                                                    STANZA 4
20   Slow down. Slow down. I am not finished teaching you.
```

## Source 1: The Mountain in the Drawer

*by Priscilla Vanterpool · Georgia Science Review, March 2024*

### The Devices We Stop Using

Open a drawer in almost any American kitchen and you will find at least one of them: a phone two models old, a tablet that will not hold a charge, a cable for a device nobody owns anymore. The average U.S. household stores eleven unused electronic items. Multiply that by 130 million households and the drawer becomes a mountain.

In 2022 the world generated 62 million metric tons of electronic waste — enough, by one estimate, to fill a line of tractor-trailers stretching around the equator. Only 22 percent of it was formally collected and recycled. The rest was stored, discarded, or handled outside any tracked system.

The material inside matters more than the volume suggests. A single smartphone contains roughly sixty different elements, including gold, cobalt, and rare-earth metals mined at real environmental cost. When a device is thrown away, every **fragment** of that material is lost from the supply and has to be mined again.

**Table 1. Electronic waste in the United States, 2023**

| Device category | Units retired (millions) | Percent formally recycled |
|---|---:|---:|
| Smartphones | 151 | 17% |
| Laptops and tablets | 62 | 24% |
| Televisions and monitors | 38 | 41% |
| Small appliances | 205 | 9% |

### Where It Goes

The cost of a discarded device is not only the lost metal. Electronics that leave the tracked system take their chemistry with them.

Lithium-ion batteries, when crushed in a compactor or buried in a landfill, can ignite; U.S. waste facilities now report more than 5,000 such fires a year. Older screens and circuit boards carry lead and flame retardants that **stream** into groundwater when their casings crack. And in places where informal recycling is common, the recovery method of last resort is fire — burning the insulation off copper wire in open air, which releases dioxins into the lungs of the people doing the work, most of whom are not paid enough to buy the devices they are taking apart.

None of this is a problem of carelessness by ordinary owners. Most people who put a phone in a drawer are doing the responsible thing as they understand it. The problem is upstream.

### A Different Way to Buy

Design decisions made years before a device reaches a drawer determine what happens to it there.

When a battery is glued rather than clipped into place, an ordinary battery replacement becomes a job requiring a heat gun, and most owners will not attempt it — so the whole device is retired when one part fails. When a case is sealed with proprietary screws, independent repair shops cannot open it, so a cracked charging port that costs four dollars to fix ends the useful life of a four-hundred-dollar machine. When manufacturers stop issuing software updates after three years, a working machine becomes unsafe to connect to a network, and a device that is physically fine is discarded anyway.

Reverse those decisions and the effects reverse with them. A device designed for repair stays in service nearly twice as long as a sealed one. Longer service means fewer units retired; fewer units retired means less material mined, less freight moved, and fewer batteries in the compactor. Repairability scores — published ratings of how easily a device can be opened, diagnosed, and fixed — now exist for most major laptop and phone models, which means a purchaser can choose for repairability the same way a purchaser chooses for battery life.

Some of the largest purchasers of electronics in the country are school districts. What they specify, manufacturers build.

The mountain in the drawer was not an accident. It was a series of design choices, and design choices can be made differently.

## Source 2: Let Us Fix It

*an editorial by Marcus Boateng · The Bellwood Register, September 8, 2024*

**1**  Last spring our school threw away forty-one Chromebooks. They were not broken in any serious sense. Thirty-eight of them needed a battery, a hinge, or a charging port.

**2**  I am not writing about forty-one laptops. I am writing because the Bellwood district is about to make a purchasing decision it will live with for six years, and almost nobody has noticed. In October the board will vote on the technology contract for the next replacement cycle. It should require that the devices we buy be repairable, and it should do it now rather than after another cycle of sealed cases.

**3**  The argument against is always the same one, and it deserves a straight answer. Some board members hold that the cheapest device is the responsible device. I understand the instinct. It is also not what the numbers show. Over six years, the sealed machine is the expensive one. Cheap is not the same as inexpensive. Cheap is a thing you buy twice.

**4**  The district has been **careful** with money for as long as I have taught here, and that instinct has served us well. But being careful about the first price and careless about the second is not thrift. It is a way of hiding a cost where the budget cannot see it.

**5**  **Although a repairable laptop costs about forty dollars more at purchase,** it stays in service nearly twice as long. A repair standard changes nothing about the machines already sitting in our closets. It asks only that what we buy next be built to open.

**6**  Meanwhile the waste is landing in our own county. The Bellwood Solid Waste Authority reported 14 tons of electronics collected at the county drop-off site last year, more than double the figure from five years ago. That is our landfill. Those are our shelves it came off of.

**7**  I have taught here nineteen years. I remember when a broken projector meant a work order and a screwdriver, not a purchase order. My students are thirteen. Most of them have never opened anything they own. Not one of them has been told that a thing which stops working can first be looked at.

**8**  That is not a nostalgic complaint. It is a measurable cost, and it is one of the few school expenses that can be reduced by a decision rather than a fundraiser. Every other budget problem we argue about takes years to fix. This one takes a specification line and a vote.

**9**  Vote for the repair standard in October.
