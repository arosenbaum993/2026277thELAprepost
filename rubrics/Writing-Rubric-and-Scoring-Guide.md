# Extended Writing Task — Rubric & Scoring Guide

**Instrument:** Georgia Milestones Grade 7 Eight-Point, Three-Trait Writing Rubric (Georgia Department of Education, August 2026 — *intended for classroom use*)
**Applies to:** Item 39 on Form A and Form B
**Maximum:** 8 points (Trait 1 = 3 · Trait 2 = 3 · Trait 3 = 2)

---

## 1. The rubric

### TRAIT 1 — Purpose and Organization (3 points)

*This trait examines the writer's ability to construct a coherent, purposeful text with a cohesive and effective organizational structure and appropriate style, written to engage a target audience. The writer uses appropriate transitions to organize ideas and information from the sources and provides a strong conclusion.*

| | **3** | **2** | **1** | **0** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Overall** | A coherent, purposeful text with a clearly stated claim and/or position, a cohesive and effective organizational structure, and an appropriate style, written to engage a target audience | A reasonably coherent and somewhat purposeful text with a claim and/or position, an adequate organizational structure, and a mostly appropriate style, written to engage an audience | An incomplete or oversimplified text with a weak organizational structure, an inconsistent style, and an unclear purpose and audience | A disorganized text with no discernible style, purpose, or audience |
| **Introduction** | Provides a strong introduction that effectively guides the focus and clearly establishes a claim and/or position | Provides an adequate introduction that guides the focus and connects to a claim and/or position | Provides a weak introduction that does little to guide the focus | Does not provide an introduction |
| **Structure** | Develops a cohesive and effective organizational structure by logically grouping ideas to convey information for a specific purpose and target audience | Develops an adequate organizational structure by grouping ideas for a mostly clear purpose and audience | Develops a weak organizational structure; has little sense of purpose and an unclear audience | Does not develop any discernible organizational structure; has no sense of purpose or audience |
| **Style** | Uses a style that is consistently appropriate for a specific purpose and target audience | Uses a style that is mostly appropriate for the purpose and audience | Uses a style that is minimally appropriate for the purpose and audience | Does not use a style that is appropriate for the purpose or audience |
| **Transitions** | Uses transition words and/or phrases effectively throughout to create cohesion within and between paragraphs; clearly connects reasons to the claim and shows relationships between ideas | Uses transitions adequately throughout, creating inconsistent cohesion within and between paragraphs; does not always make clear connections between reasons and the claim | Uses transitions ineffectively, creating minimal cohesion; makes weak connections | Does not use transition words or phrases to make connections |
| **Conclusion** | Provides a strong conclusion that effectively finalizes the response | Provides an adequate conclusion that completes the response | Provides a weak conclusion | Does not provide a conclusion |

---

### TRAIT 2 — Evidence and Elaboration (3 points)

*This trait examines the writer's ability to conduct research using well-chosen evidence from multiple sources to support claim(s) and/or a position on a topic. The writer effectively applies argumentative and expository techniques by integrating supporting facts, reasons, explanations, details, descriptions, and/or events, using the writer's own words to connect evidence to claim(s) and effectively support a position.*

| | **3** | **2** | **1** | **0** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Overall** | A well-developed text that effectively supports a position by effectively elaborating on the provided reasons, integrating relevant and well-chosen evidence from the sources, and strategically applying argumentative and expository techniques | A partially developed text that generally supports a position by adequately elaborating on the provided reasons, using relevant evidence from the sources, and applying argumentative and expository techniques | An inadequately developed text that supports a weak idea by minimally elaborating on the provided reason(s), using mostly irrelevant or poorly chosen evidence, and showing minimal use of techniques | Does not use reasons, relevant evidence from the sources, elaboration, or techniques |
| **Reasons** | Provides reasons that effectively support the stated claim and/or position | Provides reasons that adequately support the stated claim and/or position | Provides reason(s) that minimally support a weak idea | Does not provide any reasons |
| **Evidence** | Supports the reasons effectively by integrating relevant and well-chosen evidence (facts, reasons, explanations, details, descriptions, and/or events) **from multiple sources** | Supports the reasons adequately by using relevant evidence from the source(s) | Supports the reason(s) minimally by using mostly irrelevant or poorly chosen evidence | Does not provide any relevant evidence from the source(s) |
| **Elaboration** | Elaborates effectively on reasons and ideas using techniques | Elaborates adequately on reasons and ideas using techniques | Elaborates minimally on reason(s) and/or ideas | Does not elaborate on any reasons or ideas |
| **Crediting sources** | Makes a consistent attempt to credit sources | Makes an inconsistent attempt to credit source(s) | Makes a weak or minimal attempt to credit source(s) | Does not attempt to credit source(s) |

---

### TRAIT 3 — Language Usage and Conventions (2 points)

*This trait examines the writer's ability to use language and conventions to convey precise meaning while demonstrating control of syntax, sentence formation, usage, and mechanics as defined in the grade-level grammar expectations of the language standards.*

| | **2** | **1** | **0** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Overall** | Demonstrates **adequate command** of sentence structure, language usage, and conventions to convey precise meaning | Demonstrates **partial command** of sentence structure, language usage, and conventions to convey meaning | Demonstrates little or no command of sentence structure, language usage, and conventions |
| **Sentences** | Uses mostly complete sentences and a variety of sentence structures to strengthen clarity and coherence | Uses some complete sentences and a limited variety of sentence structures with minimal clarity and/or coherence | Uses few or no complete sentences; lacks clarity and coherence |
| **Verb tense** | Uses mostly correct verb tense | Uses some correct verb tense | Uses little or no correct verb tense |
| **Voice** | Uses mostly active voice to maintain consistency | Uses active voice inconsistently | Uses little or no active voice |
| **Usage & mechanics** | Contains few, if any, errors in usage and conventions; does not contain errors that interfere with meaning | Contains errors in usage and conventions that sometimes interfere with meaning | Contains frequent errors in usage and conventions that often interfere with meaning |

> **Length rule (from the rubric):** *The response must be of sufficient length to demonstrate either partial or adequate command. Very brief responses, even with few or no errors, may not be sufficient to demonstrate adequate or partial command.*
>
> Practical floor: a response under roughly 100 words rarely supports a Trait 3 score above 1, regardless of error count. A clean four-sentence paragraph has not demonstrated command of sentence variety — it has avoided the opportunity.

## 2. How to score — the actual procedure

Do this in order. It takes about 4 minutes per essay once the routine is established.

**Step 1 — Read the whole essay once without a pen.** Do not score on the first pass. Trait 1 is about the *shape* of the argument, and you cannot see shape while marking errors.

**Step 2 — Score Trait 1.** Ask, in this order: Is there a claim, and can I point to it? Do the paragraphs group ideas that belong together? Does the style suit a city council / school board? Do the transitions name real relationships? Does the ending finalize, or just stop?

**Step 3 — Score Trait 2.** Underline every piece of source evidence. Then ask: How many *different* sources? Is each piece attached to a reason? Is there a sentence *in the student's own words* explaining why that evidence matters? Is any source credited?

**Step 4 — Score Trait 3 last, and score it as a whole impression, not an error count.** The rubric asks for *command*, not perfection. Read for whether the sentences work. Then check the four sub-rows.

**Step 5 — Record all three trait scores separately in the workbook.** A single 8-point total destroys the diagnostic value.

### The three decisions that cause most scoring disagreement

**1. One source or two?** Trait 2's 3-point row says "from multiple sources." A response that uses only one source, however well, is capped at **2**. This is the most frequently missed cap and the most consequential — check it on every paper.

**2. Evidence versus elaboration.** These are different rows and are scored separately.

> *Evidence:* "Achebe reports that unshielded lighting misdirects up to 30 percent of hatchlings."
> *Elaboration:* "That means nearly one in three hatchlings from a nest beside a lit road never reaches the water — a loss the ordinance could prevent without touching a single existing building."

A paper full of accurate quotations and empty of explanation is a **2 at most** on Trait 2. Students who "quote and drop" are the largest single group in most Grade 7 classes, and the workbook's Trait 2 column is where you will find them.

**3. Crediting sources.** Any consistent signal phrase counts — *According to Achebe…*, *Whitcomb reports that…*, *(Source 1)*. Formal MLA is not required at Grade 7 (7.T.RA.2.c asks for "a standard format," and a signal phrase is one). What is *not* credit: quotation marks alone; "the article says" with no identification of which article when two are in play.

### Position neutrality

**Score the argument, never the position.** A student arguing *against* the ordinance or the repair standard can earn 3/3/2 — the sources contain real counter-evidence (cost, limited reach of a new-construction-only rule, up-front price). If you find yourself scoring the conclusion, stop and re-read Trait 2's rows: none of them mention which side the writer took.

## 3. Condition codes

Enter these instead of a score when a response cannot be rubric-scored. The workbook treats them as missing, not as zero, so they do not depress class averages or fabricate growth.

| Code | Meaning | Enter in workbook |
|---|---|---|
| **A** | Blank / no response | `A` |
| **B** | Insufficient — a copied prompt, a single sentence, or a refusal | `B` |
| **C** | Off-topic / off-purpose — does not address the prompt | `C` |
| **D** | Illegible | `D` |
| **E** | Written in a language other than English | `E` |

A condition code at pre-test paired with a real score at post-test is a genuine and reportable outcome — the workbook flags it as *"engaged at post"* rather than computing a meaningless gain.

## 4. Score-point anchors

Use these as calibration descriptors. Replace them with real student papers from your own classes after the first administration — locally collected anchors are always more useful than described ones.

### Trait 1 anchors

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **3** | Opens with background and a claim that names both the decision and the position. Each body paragraph carries one reason, and paragraphs are ordered so the strongest or most contested point lands where it will do the most work. Transitions name relationships ("Beyond the environmental cost…", "The stronger objection is…"). Style is consistently formal and addressed to the named body. The conclusion returns to the decision and gives the reader something to do or hold. |
| **2** | Claim is present but may appear at the end of the introduction as an afterthought, or may state the topic more clearly than the position. Paragraphs each hold a reason but their order feels arbitrary. Transitions are present but generic ("Also," "Another reason"). Style slips once or twice into conversational register. Conclusion restates the reasons without finalizing. |
| **1** | Reader must infer the position. Ideas from different reasons appear in the same paragraph. Few or repeated transitions. Audience is not evident from any choice on the page. Ends abruptly or with an unrelated remark. |
| **0** | No discernible structure, purpose, or audience. |

### Trait 2 anchors

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **3** | Two or more reasons, each supported by evidence from a *different* source, each followed by the writer's own explanation of why that evidence supports that reason. A counterclaim is stated fairly and answered with evidence rather than dismissal. Sources are credited by name throughout. |
| **2** | Reasons are present and supported, but evidence may come from one source only (**cap at 2**), or evidence is accurate but under-explained. Counterclaim may be mentioned without being answered. Crediting is inconsistent — one signal phrase, then three uncredited borrowings. |
| **1** | One weak idea. Evidence is thin, mostly irrelevant, or restated from the prompt. Little or no elaboration. Little or no attempt to credit. |
| **0** | No reasons, no source evidence, no elaboration. |

### Trait 3 anchors

| Score | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **2** | Sentences are complete and varied — simple, compound, and complex constructions appear and are used for effect. Tense is consistent. Voice is mostly active. Errors exist but a reader never has to stop and reconstruct a sentence. |
| **1** | Sentences are mostly complete but repetitive in structure. Tense shifts once or twice. Passive constructions accumulate. Errors occasionally force rereading. |
| **0** | Frequent fragments or run-ons; errors regularly obstruct meaning. |

## 5. Double-scoring protocol

Single-rater trait scores in Grade 7 ELA typically show 60%–70% exact agreement between teachers. That is too much noise to carry a pre/post growth claim on its own. This protocol costs about 20 minutes per class period and raises agreement substantially.

**Before scoring any student work (15 minutes, once per administration):**

1. Each teacher scores the same **five** papers independently — pick a range, not five middling ones.
2. Compare all three trait scores paper by paper.
3. Discuss only the disagreements, and resolve each by pointing at a **rubric row**, not at a general impression. "It's a 2 on Trait 2 because the elaboration row says 'adequately' and there's one explaining sentence in four paragraphs" is a resolution. "It just feels like a 3" is not.
4. Write down any rule you had to invent to resolve a disagreement. Apply it to every paper afterward, and apply it again in April.

**During scoring:**

5. Every teacher independently double-scores **20%** of another teacher's papers (every fifth paper).
6. Where trait scores differ by **1 point**, average them and record the average (the workbook accepts 0.5 increments).
7. Where they differ by **2 or more points on Trait 1 or Trait 2**, a third reader resolves. This should be rare; if it is not rare, your team has not converged and should recalibrate on five more papers.

**After scoring:**

8. Record the exact-agreement rate on the `Settings` sheet of the workbook. **Below 70%, do not report individual student essay growth** — report class-level trait movement only, and say why.

**At post-test:** use the **same anchors and the same scorers**. Scorer drift between September and April is the most common way a school accidentally manufactures — or erases — writing growth. If a different teacher must score in April, re-run step 1 with the September anchor papers before touching new work.

## 6. From trait scores to instruction

The workbook does this automatically, but the logic is worth knowing:

| Pattern across the class | What it means | Where to start |
|---|---|---|
| **Trait 1 low, Trait 2 higher** | Students have content but no architecture. | Model claim-writing and paragraph ordering. Peer Revision Guidance, Trait 1 guided questions. |
| **Trait 2 low, Trait 1 higher** | Students can build a shape but fill it with assertion. | Explicit evidence → elaboration instruction. Sentence stems: *"This matters because…"* / *"In other words…"* Items 27 and 28 on the test are the same skill and can be reused as warm-ups. |
| **Trait 3 low, others adequate** | Editing, not composing, is the gap. | Targeted GUM work — sentence combining, tense consistency, active voice. See the *K–12 GUM Chart* rows marked M at Grades 6–7. |
| **All three low but item scores in Part 3 adequate** | Students can *recognize* good writing but not *produce* it. | This is the most common Grade 7 profile and it is a **volume** problem. Increase the number of short timed writes, not the amount of instruction. |
| **All three low and Part 3 items also low** | Recognition has not been built yet. | Start with the Peer Revision Task routine on shared drafts before assigning independent composition. |

## 7. Alignment to the Peer Revision Task

The Georgia Milestones ELA assessment includes a **Peer Revision Task**, and the *Grade 7 Classroom Peer Revision Guidance* (February 2026) exists to prepare students for it. Part 3 of both forms is built from that document's sample item stems, so the same instructional routine prepares students for both Part 3 and Part 4.

**The routine, run weekly on shared or student drafts:**

1. Students construct a draft text aligned with a research question, purpose, and target audience.
2. Teacher delivers a mini-lesson on **one criterion from one trait** — not the whole rubric.
3. Brief review of the rubric row and the checklist for that criterion.
4. Students exchange drafts.
5. Students annotate and give targeted feedback using the 3-part guided questions.
6. Students discuss the feedback with their partner.
7. Students revise.

**Mapping test items to the criterion to teach:**

| Test items missed | Trait | Criterion for the mini-lesson |
|---|---|---|
| 25, 38 | 1 | Introductions that establish a claim; conclusions that finalize |
| 30 | 1 | Transitions that create cohesion within and between paragraphs |
| 31 | 1 | Style appropriate to purpose and audience |
| 26, 29 | 2 | Reasons that support the claim; counterclaims that strengthen it |
| 27, 37 | 2 | Evidence from multiple sources; crediting sources |
| 28 | 2 | Elaboration — explaining how evidence supports the reason |
| 32, 33, 34, 35, 36 | 3 | Verb tense, active voice, modifiers, punctuation, precise word choice |

The workbook's **Intervention Groups** sheet produces exactly this mapping for your actual students, grouped and ready to teach.
